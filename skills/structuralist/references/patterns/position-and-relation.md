# Pattern: Position and Relation

## Lens

Meaning depends partly on which system it belongs to, where it sits there, and
how it relates to other meaning. Position can affect authority, visibility,
interpretation, navigation, and expected change. Relations can express
dependency, refinement, contrast, sequence, evidence, governance, realization,
or another local connection.

## Tension

Centralizing meaning can make authority clear but overload one location.
Distributing it can serve several contexts but create ambiguity or drift.
Explicit relations aid traversal and change reasoning; too many relations turn
the system into topology that readers must maintain without benefit.

## Questions

- Where do readers currently look for this meaning, and why?
- Is this meaning acting on the project, realizing an artifact, supplying
  evidence, or genuinely doing more than one of these?
- At which project-relative boundary should it be interpreted and changed?
- Is authority concentrated, distributed, contested, or intentionally plural?
- Which direction of realization or dependency does this meaning participate
  in, and which consequence—rather than which whole document—should move?
- Is lower-level evidence pressuring an upstream owner, or silently replacing
  its authority because the realized behavior already differs?
- Which relations materially change how an element should be read or edited?
- Are different meanings sharing one owner even though their authorities,
  change cadences, audiences, disclosure paths, or downstream consequences
  require independent positions?
- Which important relation is hidden, and which visible relation has become
  incidental?
- Would moving, linking, or reframing the meaning alter its role?

## Possible movements

Centralize, distribute, move, link, order, subordinate, make adjacent, decouple,
bridge, or leave a relation implicit. A clear current owner is often useful,
but it is a response to an authority problem rather than a universal file rule.

Reframe or move a boundary when artifact semantics have been mistaken for
project-system authority, or when a genuine dual role has been hidden by a
false separation. The same file can occupy different positions relative to
different systems; state which relation matters for the task.

A formalization gradient is normally a partial order, not a single ladder.
Keep downward realization, upward evidence, peer plurality, and cross-cutting
governance distinguishable instead of forcing every carrier into one level.

When an owner is structurally overloaded, reposition only the meanings that
need independent change and reconnect the resulting owners to their governing
and realized consequences. Do not treat creating files as the repair itself:
an unnecessary split can replace overload with drift and navigation cost.

Use navigation, synthesis, and structural metadata only when they help readers
perceive real relations. They must not acquire authority merely by presenting
other material.
